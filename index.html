I built a three-step lead waterfall. It filters out leads that almost never buy, scores the rest, and sends each one to sales or tags it for a watch list or self-serve. On the last six months of past leads, held out from training, it found 63 good leads where HubSpot's MQL rule found 45. Both sent sales 118 leads. All 490 newer leads are graded, each with a rep line, in this Google Sheet.

Three-step lead scoring waterfall

Filter outFree email, reward recipients asking for help, mobile signups
ScoreLogistic regression trained on past leads
Route and gradeSales, watch list or self-serve, with a rep line
  1. Filter out free email, reward recipients asking for help, and mobile signups. These leads almost never buy. Free email leads reached $10K 0.2% of the time, recipients asking about a gift card 0.8%, and mobile signups 0 of 72. Recipients go straight to support.
  2. Score the rest with a logistic regression trained on past leads. It gives each lead a probability of reaching $10K, based on 12 fields led by stated volume, job title, use case and company size.
  3. Route and grade. The top 30% of scores go to sales (A), the next 30% to the watch list (B), and the rest to self-serve (C). Each lead gets a rep line with the next step and the two fields that moved its score most.

Waterfall vs. HubSpot's grade

I tested on 477 leads from Apr–Sep 2024 that the model never saw during training. 106 of them reached $10K.

The simple rule sends any lead that passes the filter and stated $10K or more a year. That one question already beats HubSpot, 60 good leads to 45. The waterfall adds 3 more at the same volume, and 8 more at the grade A cutoff. Method in Appendix D.

To find HubSpot's 45 good leads, the waterfall sends 60 leads instead of 118. It found more good leads than HubSpot in 99.6% of 2,000 resamples of the test months.

On the 490, it sends sales 112 leads where HubSpot would send 121, and the two agree on 62. If the 490 behave like the test months, the A grade holds about 56 good leads and HubSpot's MQLs about 46.

Why it works

HubSpot's number comes from the lead's first action, and its letter mostly tracks whether the company record is filled in. B1 and B2 leads reached $10K at almost the same rate (26.2% and 25.6%), but only B1 becomes an MQL. Of the 222 good leads HubSpot never sent, 187 were B2s.

The waterfall scores what a lead says about its size. Leads that stated $10K or more a year reached $10K 45–52% of the time, against 19.8% overall. More signals in Appendix E.

What could make these results look too good:

  • The target account list was exported after these outcomes, so I left it out. With it, the test finds 91 good leads instead of 63, which is too good to trust.
  • No column shows which leads sales worked, so I can't measure what a rep adds.
  • I chose the filter rules after seeing all 2,015 leads. In the test months, filtered leads still rarely bought (1 good lead of 112).
  • Mobile signups rose from 3.6% to 9.4% of leads. If mobile tracking changed, recheck the mobile filter.
  • The data is synthetic, so some patterns may be planted.

Rollout and next tests

On the 490, the A cutoff sends 16 leads a month, close to HubSpot's 17. If sales has room for more, lower the cutoff; the ranking doesn't change.

I'd run both grades side by side and keep HubSpot's grade live until one system clearly wins. A good lead can take 18 months to show, so I'd judge the run on first orders within 6 months. In the test months, first orders ranked the two systems the same way $10K did.

  1. Month 1: log both grades, route as today, and ask reps if the rep lines help.
  2. Months 2–7: sales works every lead either system sends, about 24 a month. Compare first-order rates on the leads only one system sends. Six test months gave 74 waterfall-only and 56 HubSpot-only leads, which ordered at 56.8% and 30.4%.
  3. Retire HubSpot's grade only if the waterfall-only leads still order at a clearly higher rate, one that holds up when the leads are resampled. If the gap is small, keep both for another quarter. After that, HubSpot's score can keep running in the background as a benchmark for each retrain.
  4. Give a random 1 in 5 B leads a rep call, to measure what a rep adds.
  5. Add ad platform once its leads have outcomes. It's filled on 17% of the 490 and no past leads.

What I'd need from Tremendous: sales-accepted and meeting-held fields in HubSpot, first-order date and amount, and rep time per lead.

C. How HubSpot grades leads today

The number follows conversion type on all 2,015 leads.

The letter leans on data completeness. Leads with no employee count get a C 85% of the time, free-email leads 95%. It still sorts leads well (A 41.8%, B 25.1%, C 1.9% reached $10K).

33.6% of A1, A2 and B1 leads reached $10K (177 of 527). 55.6% of the leads that reached $10K were never MQLs.

The decline in MQL quality shows up only mildly in this data. MQLs reached $10K 43.5% of the time in 2022 H2 and 29.5–39.0% in each half-year since. All leads held at 17.5–21.6%.

D. How the test was run
  • Split by time: trained on leads before Apr 2024 and tested on Apr–Sep 2024, the way the grade will be used.
  • Split by company: companies that appear in the test months were dropped from training, so the model can't score a lead by recognizing its company.
  • Compared at the same volume: each system sends 118 test leads, so a higher hit rate can't come from sending fewer.
  • Fixed the cutoffs in advance from training scores: A is the top 30% of unfiltered training leads (score 0.296 or higher), B the next 30%. They were applied unchanged to the test months.
  • Resampled the test leads 2,000 times to see how often the waterfall still wins. The gain ranged from 5 to 32 extra good leads in 95% of resamples.
  • The grades for the 490 come from the same setup refit on all 2,015 past leads, so the shipped weights differ slightly from the tested ones.
